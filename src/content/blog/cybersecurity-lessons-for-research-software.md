Before focusing on quantum algorithms, I worked in Cybersecurity Risk Consulting at PwC India. The technical domains are different, but the habits transfer surprisingly well.

Cybersecurity taught me that a large volume of data is not the same as evidence, a control is not useful merely because it exists, and a system should be designed with failure in mind. Those ideas now shape how I build research software.

## Risk needs context

A vulnerability does not have the same consequence in every organization. Risk depends on the asset, exposure, threat, existing controls, and business impact.

Research software has the same problem. A numerical error means different things in:

- a teaching notebook;
- an exploratory simulation;
- a benchmark used in a paper;
- a package used by another laboratory;
- a workflow supporting a physical experiment.

The level of testing and documentation should match the consequence of failure.

NIST’s [Cybersecurity Framework 2.0](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/02/nist-releases-version-20-landmark-cybersecurity-framework) organizes risk management around Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover. I use a smaller research version:

| Security function | Research-software equivalent                           |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Govern            | Define the claim, owner, and acceptable error          |
| Identify          | Map data, dependencies, assumptions, and failure modes |
| Protect           | Validate inputs and control changes                    |
| Detect            | Monitor invariants, drift, and failed experiments      |
| Respond           | Preserve evidence and diagnose the cause               |
| Recover           | Reproduce the last trusted result                      |

## Logs are not conclusions

Security platforms can generate more alerts than a team can investigate. The solution is not always more collection. It is better prioritization, context, and evidence.

Research pipelines also produce large logs, optimizer histories, intermediate energies, and backend metadata. Saving everything without structure can still make a result impossible to interpret.

I separate:

- **raw evidence:** measurements, configurations, and job outputs;
- **derived evidence:** energies, uncertainties, and benchmark tables;
- **decisions:** why a run was accepted, rejected, or repeated.

Google’s Site Reliability Engineering guidance distinguishes monitoring, dashboards, alerts, and root causes in its chapter on [monitoring distributed systems](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/). The principle I borrow is simple: collect signals that support an action.

An optimizer callback should not only print numbers. It should help answer whether convergence is stable, whether uncertainty is shrinking, and why the run stopped.

## A passing control may still be weak

In cybersecurity, a control can be documented and still fail to reduce the actual risk. A policy may exist without being followed. A tool may run without covering the important assets.

In research, a test can pass while proving very little.

Weak tests include:

- checking that a function returns a number;
- comparing an output with a value generated by the same implementation;
- using a tolerance so wide that a broken result passes;
- testing only equilibrium geometries;
- testing only noiseless simulators;
- ignoring warnings and non-convergence.

A useful test is tied to a failure mode. If I am worried about energy offsets, I test electronic and total energies separately. If I am worried about unit confusion, I test equivalent angstrom and bohr inputs. If I am worried about optimizer instability, I test several seeds.

## Make changes traceable

Security investigations depend on timelines: what changed, who changed it, and what happened next.

For research code, I want the same traceability:

- version-control commit;
- resolved configuration;
- dependency versions;
- input checksums;
- start and end timestamps;
- backend job identifiers;
- a machine-readable result.

This is not bureaucracy. It is the shortest path from a surprising graph back to the experiment that produced it.

## Design for detection and recovery

Perfect prevention is unrealistic. Good systems detect problems and recover safely.

For a long quantum workflow, that means:

- checkpoint optimizer state;
- make jobs idempotent where possible;
- avoid overwriting raw results;
- validate files before aggregation;
- retry transient failures with limits;
- preserve failed-run metadata;
- resume from a verified checkpoint;
- generate reports from saved data rather than rerunning experiments.

Google SRE’s discussion of [error budgets](https://sre.google/sre-book/service-best-practices/) treats reliability and development speed as a measurable trade-off. Research has a similar trade-off. Early exploration can tolerate more failure. A published benchmark should have a much smaller error budget.

## Evidence should support a decision

In consulting, technical findings are useful when they help someone choose an action. Research reporting benefits from the same discipline.

Instead of:

> The VQE energy was -1.1372 Ha.

I prefer:

> Across ten seeded runs, the median absolute error was 0.8 mHa against exact diagonalization; two runs exceeded 2 mHa after the optimizer reached its iteration limit.

The second statement tells us what to investigate.

## Keep current threat data in perspective

Industry reports can show what is happening across many organizations, but they should not be applied blindly to one environment. Verizon’s [2026 Data Breach Investigations Report](https://www.verizon.com/business/resources/T343/reports/2026-dbir-data-breach-investigations-report.pdf) reports vulnerability exploitation as the most common initial-access vector in its dataset. That is a population-level observation, not proof that every organization has the same highest risk.

Research benchmarks deserve the same caution. Average performance over a dataset does not guarantee reliability on a specific molecule, geometry, or device.

## My practical checklist

Before trusting a research workflow, I ask:

1. What decision will this result support?
2. What are the important failure modes?
3. Which controls address each failure?
4. How will a failure be detected?
5. Is the raw evidence preserved?
6. Can the result be traced to code and configuration?
7. Can the workflow recover without corrupting prior results?
8. Are limitations visible to the next user?

## The practical lesson

Cybersecurity made me less impressed by systems that only work when everything goes right.

Reliable research software assumes that configurations will be wrong, dependencies will change, optimizers will fail, hardware will drift, and people will misunderstand interfaces. The job is to make those failures visible, bounded, and recoverable.
