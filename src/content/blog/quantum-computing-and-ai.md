Quantum computing and artificial intelligence are often placed together because both are advancing quickly. That is not enough to make the combination useful.

The meaningful question is narrower: where does one technology remove a real bottleneck for the other?

I see three different directions:

1. quantum computing for machine learning;
2. AI for building and operating quantum systems;
3. AI and quantum computing as components of a scientific workflow.

They should be evaluated separately.

## Quantum computing for machine learning

Quantum machine learning usually places a quantum subroutine inside a classical learning pipeline. Common examples include quantum kernels, parameterized quantum circuits, quantum generative models, and quantum linear-algebra routines.

The 2017 [Nature review by Biamonte and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature23474) organized many of these ideas and helped establish the field. The promise comes from quantum state spaces, interference, and algorithms that may manipulate some structures efficiently.

The practical obstacles are just as important.

### Data loading

Most business and scientific datasets begin as classical numbers. Encoding a large classical vector into a quantum state may cost enough to remove an apparent speedup. Any end-to-end comparison must include data preparation and readout.

### Trainability

Parameterized circuits can develop barren plateaus, regions where gradients become too small to support efficient training. The [Nature Computational Science review on QML challenges](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43588-022-00311-3) covers trainability, data encoding, noise, benchmarking, and the need for realistic applications.

### Classical competition

Classical machine learning is fast-moving, well optimized, and supported by mature hardware. A quantum model should be compared with strong classical baselines of similar parameter count and tuning effort.

### Readout

A quantum state may contain a large amount of information, but extracting that information requires measurement. A proposal that assumes full access to every amplitude is not an operational algorithm.

## Where a rigorous speedup exists

In 2021, Liu, Arunachalam, and Temme proved an end-to-end speedup for a specific supervised-learning problem using a quantum kernel. IBM’s first-party explanation is unusually careful about the boundaries: the constructed data has a special structure related to the discrete logarithm problem, and the method is beyond current useful hardware for the relevant cases. The result and caveats are summarized by [IBM Research](https://research.ibm.com/blog/quantum-kernels), with the peer-reviewed paper linked there.

This is how I prefer to discuss QML:

- a mathematically clear task;
- an explicit data-access model;
- a strong classical comparison;
- a statement of hardware requirements;
- no assumption that one constructed advantage applies to everyday datasets.

## AI for quantum computing

The reverse direction may deliver value sooner. Quantum systems require repeated decisions involving noisy data:

- calibration and control;
- pulse optimization;
- noise characterization;
- circuit compilation;
- decoder design;
- experiment scheduling;
- anomaly detection;
- materials and device design.

These are natural areas for optimization and machine learning because data is generated by the quantum hardware itself. The input problem is therefore different from loading a large external dataset into amplitudes.

An emerging example is AI-assisted search for quantum error-correcting codes. IBM Research reported an LLM-guided evolutionary workflow that generated many candidate code constructions in 2026. IBM also states clearly that further work is required before the candidates can be considered useful. That distinction between proposing candidates and validating codes is essential; the [IBM Research article](https://research.ibm.com/blog/ai-for-qec) should be read as an industry research update, not a finished engineering result.

## A shared scientific workflow

The combination I find most practical is not “replace AI with a quantum neural network.” It is a pipeline in which each method performs the part suited to it.

For molecular discovery, a future workflow might look like:

```text
large candidate library
  → classical AI screening
  → physics-based filtering
  → high-accuracy classical chemistry
  → quantum calculation for selected hard cases
  → experimental validation
  → model update
```

AI narrows the search space. Classical simulation handles established regimes. A quantum solver is reserved for instances where electronic correlation creates a genuine classical bottleneck. Experiments remain the final judge.

AlphaFold is a useful example of AI changing a scientific workflow without eliminating experiments. The peer-reviewed [AlphaFold paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03819-2) reported atomic-accuracy predictions for many CASP14 targets and validated the model in a blind community assessment. Demis Hassabis has described AlphaFold as evidence that AI can accelerate scientific discovery, but the work also provides confidence estimates and known limitations rather than treating every prediction as equally reliable.

Quantum-science workflows will need the same discipline.

## A benchmark I would trust

Suppose a quantum model is proposed for molecular-property prediction. I would ask for:

1. a public dataset and fixed split;
2. strong classical baselines;
3. equal treatment of hyperparameter tuning;
4. ablation of the quantum component;
5. total runtime including encoding and measurement;
6. performance over several seeds;
7. scaling with dataset and qubit count;
8. noise-aware hardware results;
9. an explanation of which data structure the quantum circuit exploits;
10. results on held-out chemistry, not only random splits.

If removing the quantum component produces the same result, the contribution is not quantum.

## What I would build now

Today I would prioritize:

- AI-assisted experiment configuration;
- learned noise or calibration models;
- surrogate models that reduce expensive quantum evaluations;
- adaptive shot allocation;
- automated search over ansätze with physical constraints;
- tools that detect drift and failed hardware jobs;
- hybrid scientific pipelines with clear handoffs.

I would treat broad claims of generic QML advantage cautiously until they survive end-to-end classical comparisons.

## The practical lesson

Quantum computing and AI can work together, but “quantum + AI” is not a method.

The useful unit is a tested workflow with a specific bottleneck. The quantum component should earn its place. The AI component should earn its place. If a simpler classical method is better, that is the method I would use.
